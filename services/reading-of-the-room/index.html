A reading of the room.

A short, standalone engagement. Before any design begins, we spend a working week reading your office for light, air, quiet and material, then write you a plain account of what is wrong and a ranked set of moves the room is asking for.

ForCompanies who suspect their office is the problem, and want to know what it is before committing to a project.

Most offices do not need redesigning. They need reading.

A reading is the smallest thing we do, and often the most useful. We come in for a working week, measure what the room is actually doing to the people in it, and leave you a written document you can act on with or without us.

It is honest by design. If the answer is paint, new acoustic treatment and a better planting regime, we will tell you that, and you will not have paid for a restructure you did not need. If the answer is bigger, you will know precisely how much bigger, and why, before a single drawing exists.

Every full restructure we do begins here. So the reading is never wasted work. If you go on to a project, its fee comes off the design stage.

How a reading is done

Five days on site, one document at the end. No drawings, no mood boards, no pitch.

01

We measure the climate, not the look

We log daylight at desk height across the floor plate at three times of day, looking for the dead centre where it falls below 300 lux and the glare zones where it spikes past 2,000. We take CO2 readings through a full working day in the rooms people complain about, because a meeting room that drifts above 1,000 ppm by mid-afternoon is a room that makes people slow and irritable, and nobody ever blames the air. We map reverberation and ambient noise, looking for the open-plan zones sitting near 55 to 60 dB where focused work quietly stops happening.

02

We read the materials in the room

We go surface by surface and ask what each one is doing. Hard plaster, sealed concrete and full-height glass make a room loud and cold long before anyone names it. We note which surfaces could be carrying acoustic and thermal work and are instead doing nothing. We check the planting you already have: whether it is specified for the light it actually sits in, whether it is alive or merely not yet dead, and whether it was ever chosen to move air rather than fill a corner.

03

We watch how the room is used

We sit in it. We see which desks people avoid, which corner everyone takes their calls in, where the team clusters at 4pm and where they never go. A floor plan tells you how a room was meant to work. A week of watching tells you how it actually works, and the gap between those two things is usually the whole problem.

04

We write the reading

You receive a written document, fifteen to twenty pages, in plain English. It opens with what the room is doing to the people in it, in sensory terms anyone can feel. Then a ranked list of moves, each with the readings behind it, what it would change, a rough cost band and the order to do them in. Quick wins that cost little and change a lot sit at the top. The structural moves that need a project sit lower, clearly marked as such.

A reading measures what a room does to the people in it, then ranks what to change first.

Useful on its own. Also where every restructure begins.

We built this engagement because we kept meeting companies who had been quoted for a full fit-out when their actual problem was three rooms running short of air and an open plan that had never been treated for sound. They did not need a project. They needed someone to tell them the truth about the room.

So a reading stands alone. Plenty of offices are good rooms being let down by two or three fixable things, and the reading finds them, ranks them, and sends you off to fix them. You are not obliged to work with us afterwards, and we have written readings for spaces we never touched again.

When a reading does lead to a restructure, nothing is repeated. The week we spent measuring becomes the foundation of the design, the baseline we improve against, and the reason the brief is right from the first drawing. The fee for the reading comes off the design stage, so you are never paying twice to understand the same room.

Included · what you get

What you receive

The written reading

A fifteen to twenty page document, in plain language, that you own outright. It states what is wrong, why, and what each fix would change. You can act on it yourself, hand it to your own contractor, or bring it back to us. It is yours either way.

The ranked moves

Every recommendation ordered by what it changes per pound spent, from a planting regime and acoustic panels you could action next week, to the structural light and air work that needs a full restructure. Each move carries its measured evidence and a cost band, so nothing on the list is a guess.

The measured baseline

Your daylight, air and acoustic readings recorded against recognised benchmarks, the WELL air and light thresholds among them, so you can see exactly where the room sits today and have something to measure any future work against.

A walkthrough conversation

We talk you through the reading in person or on a call, answer the awkward questions, and help you decide what is worth doing now, what can wait, and whether the room needs fixing or redesigning.

What you walk away with

  • 01A clear, evidenced answer to whether your office needs fixing or redesigning, before you commit to either.
  • 02A ranked list of moves you can start on next week, with the cheap, high-impact ones at the top.
  • 03A measured baseline of light, air and quiet, so you can prove what any future work actually changed.
Questions · before you ask
How long does a reading take, and how disruptive is it?

We are on site across a normal working week, roughly five days, because the measurements that matter only show up while the office is being used. There is no construction and nothing to clear. We log light, air and sound in the background while your team works as usual. You receive the written reading about a week after we leave.

Do I have to commit to a full restructure afterwards?

No. A reading is deliberately standalone. Many offices need fixing rather than redesigning, and if that is yours, the document will say so and send you off to do it, with us or without us. If you do go on to a full restructure, the reading fee comes off the design stage, so the work is never paid for twice.

What exactly do you measure?

Daylight in lux at desk height across the floor and through the day, CO2 in parts per million in the rooms people actually use, reverberation and ambient noise in decibels, and the condition of any existing planting and surfaces. We record everything against recognised benchmarks, including the WELL air and light thresholds, so the readings mean something beyond our opinion.

Can I use the reading with my own architect or contractor?

Yes. The document is written in plain English and you own it. Plenty of clients take a reading straight to the team they already have. The ranked moves and the measured baseline are exactly what a good designer or contractor needs to scope the work properly.
