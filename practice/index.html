On method

We work the way gardeners work, not the way architects do. Slow, attentive, in seasons — rooms grown rather than rooms drawn.

Most offices were planned for a different climate than the one people now live in. Light got blocked. Air got dried. Sound got hard. We restructure that climate first, then the floor plan follows from it — not the other way around.

01

We start with the air, then the floor plan follows.

Every restructure begins with a three-day climate study. We measure CO₂, particulate matter, surface humidity, daylight angle, ambient noise at three times of day. None of that goes into a marketing report. It goes into the brief.

By the time we sketch a single wall, we know which corners are dead, which windows are wasted, where the building is fighting the people inside it. Floor plans drawn this way feel obvious, not clever.

The team is small on purpose. A botanist, a daylight specialist, two interior architects, an acoustician we trust. Anything beyond that and the room stops being a room and starts being a deliverable.

We do not work on more than four restructures a quarter. Slower work, fewer rooms, deeper attention — that is the only scale we are interested in.

02

Plants are infrastructure, not decoration.

We do not buy plants from showrooms. We grow a planting plan the same way a quiet courtyard is grown — with shade tolerance, root depth, water rhythm, leaf density. Every room gets a planting diagram before it gets a furniture plan.

The plants do work. They soften reverb, drop dry-air complaints, hold humidity inside a room overnight. None of that is symbolic. It is measured before and after, every project.

Maintenance is part of the design, not an afterthought billed by someone else. We hand over a care calendar, train the office manager, and check on every plant for the first six months — on us.

A space that loses its plants in eight weeks was never restructured. It was decorated. That is the line we hold.

03

Quiet is a material we design with on purpose.

Most modern offices are loud the way modern food is sweet — by default, by accident, and by industry standard. Glass walls, plaster ceilings, polished floors. The acoustics are no one's fault and everyone's problem.

We design the soft surfaces first: lime plaster ceilings, oak slat panels, dense planting walls, wool rugs cut to room geometry. The result is a room you can think inside without raising your voice.

For meeting rooms we draw two acoustic zones — one for presentation, one for conversation. Different absorption, different reverb, different intent. We tune them with a noise-source test, not a vibe.

The cheapest way to make a room feel expensive is to make it sound quiet. We have stopped being shy about that.
